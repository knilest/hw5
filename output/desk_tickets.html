Reflection

All evidence below comes from the three ticket tabs, the Cash tab, and the audit trail of the full run on 2026-10-09 (run ids e1fb9ffc1472, cfe6176e9f76, 2b69a2a592b9).

1. How well did the agents perform on each ticket?

Overall, the team did a good job. Every ticket was resolved, every dollar went through a human signature, no rule was broken, and the ending balance ($152.00) matches the database to the cent. The agents worked together well: hand-offs carried the right facts, and each specialist stayed in its lane. Where I'd push them is efficiency. The Boss sometimes took extra steps that didn't add anything, and a team should feel faster than one agent, which it didn't always.

TicketGradeWhat went wellWhat I'd improve
101
Bulldog tee		 A Textbook routing: Inventory first (size S = 0, other sizes in stock, vendor blocked by invoice 501), then Accounting with those facts (the $840 invoice payment and an $8 PO for exactly the 1-unit shortfall), then Customer Service. The Boss gave the approvals in the only order that works: invoice first, because the PO would be refused while the vendor was still blocked. Tauhid's draft offered real options (M/L/XL) without promising a date or mentioning the unpaid invoice. Fast: about 34 seconds. Redundant lookups. Four agents called get_ticket and three called get_shop_today, and Customer Service re-checked sizes Inventory had already reported. Each specialist starts fresh, so the team pays a "re-read the file" cost on every hand-off. The Boss also took 9 model turns, more than any specialist (Accounting 5, Inventory 3, Customer Service 3).
102
Rent due		 A− The cleanest delegation of the run. The Boss called only Facilities; Facilities verified the landlord and the "due in 2 days" claim against lease 1 and handed the money part to Accounting itself. That's a real two-level hand-off, and it shows the team respecting separation of duties: the agent who checks the bill isn't the one who requests payment. Slowest ticket despite being the simplest: 199 seconds. The audit trail shows the specialists finished in about 23 seconds, and 162 seconds was one stalled model response on the Boss's final wrap-up. That's API latency, not bad teamwork, but it means one slow call on a single "manager" agent can hold up the whole ticket. Both Facilities and Accounting also pulled the same lease.
103
Bulk hoodie discount		 B+ The hardest judgment call, handled sensibly. Inventory found 8 of 20 size M. Accounting proposed $46.00 (20.7% off, $24 margin per hoodie, $480 profit), and instead of drafting a $264 restock PO the shop couldn't pay for, it flagged the $112 shortfall and let me decide. Inventory and Accounting ran in parallel, which is the speed-up a team should give. The AI Club draft said the discount was "under review" rather than promising it. The Boss asked Customer Service a second time just to confirm the draft existed, even though it had the board item id (#6) and a list_board_items tool to check for itself. That added a whole extra Customer Service run (it re-read the ticket, the board, and the stock) and tokens for nothing. Also, the discount was proposed for all 20 hoodies when only 8 can be filled today, so I approved a price for units we can't deliver without a restock we can't afford. A sharper Accounting agent would have priced the 8 available units, or flagged that the 20-unit price depends on the restock.

What I'd change next time. I want the agents to be thorough the first time, so no second pass is needed. The Boss should trust the board over a re-ask (its prompt already says "trust the board," but it didn't follow that on 103). Hand-off requests should carry enough context that specialists don't re-fetch the ticket and date. And independent work should run in parallel, as it did on 103. The team used 55 model requests and about 207,000 tokens across three tickets, and the Boss accounted for 25 of those requests (45%). The manager layer is the biggest cost center, so that's where the efficiency gains are.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools?

My honest take: the team was the right design for this shop overall, but ticket 102 would have been simpler, cheaper, and probably faster as one agent, and parts of 101 too.

  • Ticket 102 is a straight line. Read ticket → get_lease → compare to today → request_payment. One agent with those four tools could do it in about four tool calls and one short conversation. The team used 13 model requests and ~43,600 tokens across three agents, and Facilities and Accounting both fetched the same lease. Every hand-off means the next agent starts with no memory, re-reads the facts, and writes a report the caller then has to read. For a task with no judgment calls, that's overhead.
  • The manager layer is the biggest cost. 45% of all model requests were the Boss routing, checking the board, and wrapping up, not doing shop work. One agent skips the "explain the task to a colleague, then read their report" step entirely.
  • Speed is the trade-off I noticed most. A team is only faster when work runs in parallel, which happened on 103 (Inventory and Accounting overlapped). Chains like 102's (Boss → Facilities → Accounting) are sequential by nature, so each layer adds latency, and one slow call on the Boss stalled the ticket for 162 seconds.
  • Safety wouldn't have suffered. The important guardrails (no negative cash, vendor-blocked POs, discount floor, human-only approval) live in the MCP server's code, not in the agent structure. A single agent with the same tools would hit the same walls.

Where the team clearly earned its keep:

  • Information walls. Customer Service never sees cash or invoices, so it can't leak "we haven't paid our printer" into Tauhid's message. One all-powerful agent would have to rely on a prompt promise not to.
  • Separation of duties. On 102, the agent that verified the bill (Facilities) wasn't the one that asked for the money (Accounting), the same control a real business uses against fraud.
  • Focused judgment on 103. Pricing, stock, and customer tone are three different skills; separate prompts let each one be detailed without one giant prompt trying to cover everything.
  • A readable audit trail. The floor view shows who decided what, which made the Expected vs Actual comparison above possible.

Best of both: route simple, single-domain tickets (rent, a paid invoice, an in-stock order) to one capable agent, and bring in the full team only when a ticket crosses money, stock, and customer words, like 101 and 103.

  1. The Yale AI Club answers our draft: "We'll take the 8 in M, and make the other 12 size L." This is the natural follow-up to 103. Inventory runs check_stock (L has 14, so 12 fits), Accounting re-uses the approved logic with propose_discount at $46 for 20 units now that all 20 can actually ship, and Customer Service drafts a confirmation. No restock and no cash needed, which matters with only $152 in checking.
  2. Restocking the Crest Mug, which is at 0. check_stock shows CC-MUG-CREST at 0. list_vendors matches Elm City Gifts ("mugs and small goods", 3-day lead) and shows it can ship (no open invoices). At $3.50 unit cost, Accounting can draft_purchase_order for 20 mugs = $70.00, which fits inside $152 with $82 to spare, and the human approves it in the in-tray. Every tool already exists, and the no-negative-cash rule caps the order size.
  3. A customer asks for 10 tees at $7.00 each "because we're a club." The tee costs $8.00, so propose_discount refuses anything at or below cost. The Boss marks the ticket declined with the reason, Accounting suggests the lowest sensible price above cost, and Customer Service drafts a polite counter-offer. That's a full cycle using guardrails already built into the server.

5. Three new problems this team could not solve, and what it would take

  1. The next rent payment: $2,400 due 2026-10-02 with $152 in the bank. After ticket 102, the lease's next due date moved to 2026-10-02, and the Cash tab shows $152.00 left. Facilities and Accounting could prepare the request, but the pay tool would (correctly) refuse it. The team has no way to bring money in: there's no revenue, no record of the AI Club's 8 hoodies sold at $46 ($368), no receivables, no forecast.
    What I'd add: a record_sale tool and a sales table (selling stock should raise cash and lower inventory), a forecast_cash(days) tool that projects upcoming rent, invoices, and expected sales, and a Treasury/Finance agent that warns the Boss weeks ahead ("rent in 32 days, projected shortfall $2,248") and proposes options (push sales, delay optional POs, ask the landlord for terms). Each option would still go to a human for approval.
  2. Goods actually arriving and leaving the shelf. On 101 we paid $8 for a size S tee "arriving in about 5 days," but nothing in the system tracks that shipment. Inventory still shows 0, nothing reserves the tee for Tauhid, and when the AI Club picks up 8 hoodies the count won't drop. Today, a paid PO is just a payment row.
    What I'd add: a purchase_orders table with status (ordered → shipped → received), plus receive_shipment(po_id), reserve_stock(ticket_id, sku, size, qty), and fulfill_order(ticket_id) tools that update inventory.qty, and a Receiving/Fulfillment agent that checks expected arrival dates against shop today and reopens tickets when goods land ("Tauhid's tee is here, so draft the pickup note").
  3. Actually talking to customers and hearing back. Both drafts I approved (to Tauhid and the AI Club) are still sitting on the board, because "approve" doesn't send anything by design. Both messages ask the customer a question (switch sizes or wait? take 8 now?), and the team has no way to receive the answer, so each ticket stops at a dead end until a human copies and pastes.
    What I'd add: a send_message(board_item_id) tool that only works on human-approved drafts (keeping the "no unapproved outreach" rule), an inbox tool that turns customer replies into new tickets linked to the original, and a customers table with contact details and order history. A Communications agent would match replies to open tickets and hand them back to the Boss, with privacy rules so customer contact info never ends up in prompts it doesn't need to.
